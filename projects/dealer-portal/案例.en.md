## 2. Fast Context

E-Bike Service Tool helps dealer technicians diagnose, maintain, and configure e-bikes. As the sole UI/UX designer across Dealer Portal, I worked with product and engineering to replace a gated service flow with a task-oriented experience. This case focuses on Service Tool: making vehicle condition visible while keeping service decisions with technicians.

- **Product:** E-Bike Service Tool
- **My role:** Product Designer
- **Timeline:** Q2 2023–present; launched March 2025
- **Team:** 1 PO, 1 Scrum Master, 2 Frontend, 3 Backend, 3 QA
- **Focus:** Enterprise Workflows · Information Architecture · Hardware Interaction



## 3. Problem Framing

### Technicians needed the picture before choosing the repair

The previous tool led technicians through checks and updates before reaching the dashboard. A problem early in that sequence could delay their understanding of the bike’s wider service needs.

#### One issue could hold up the overview

Technicians could encounter a blocking issue before seeing other problems, making it harder to plan repairs or explain the work to customers.

#### Guidance was difficult to follow across sources

Troubleshooting instructions were constrained by pop-ups, while additional help lived in manuals, videos, and support channels.

#### Component data overshadowed service priorities

The dashboard emphasized serial numbers and firmware versions. Diagnostic tools and help resources were less prominent than the information technicians needed for their next action.

## 4. Strategic Direction

### Automate diagnosis, keep service decisions with technicians

We separated discovering problems from deciding how to address them. The system would diagnose what it could, surface the findings, and explain the next actions. Technicians would choose and initiate updates, maintenance, and repairs, subject to necessary technical dependencies.

This kept vehicle changes visible to dealers and their customers. Guidance also needed to explain how the system worked, so technicians could build understanding while servicing the bike.


## 5. Key Decisions

### Diagnosis — Gather findings before asking technicians to fix them

The old sequence could require an issue to be resolved before continuing. In Full Diagnosis, we kept the necessary checking order but recorded detected issues and continued the inspection. Results were grouped before technicians returned to Workspace to address them.

I worked with the team to organize checks into four categories that technicians could understand and engineering could support. Some checks still required physical input. This separated diagnosis from repair decisions without pretending the hardware had no dependencies.

### Workspace — Make current tasks the starting point

Our Beta already combined search, tools, and Workspace. Feedback from our largest brand customer showed that technicians’ path through those features was still unclear. Other layout explorations exposed a tension: emphasizing tasks could hide tools, while showing every tool weakened task priority.

I proposed the three-column layout and refined it with the team. Tools remained on the left, vehicle condition occupied the center, and current tasks stayed on the right. Historical records moved to Service Book. Tasks were graded by their effect on riding, while “To Be Diagnosed” kept incomplete checks visible. Technicians could follow a task into its handling content or choose a tool directly.

### Service Guidance — Explain the problem, then support the next action

A task needed to lead somewhere useful. I established a three-part content template: Description explains normal operation, Potential Impact explains what could happen if an issue remains, and Troubleshooting guides technicians through possible causes.

Working with marketing on classification and writing depth, I designed the structure so support teams could extend steps, add media, and include diagnostic modules. This balanced a consistent reading path with different repair needs.

When self-service was insufficient, technicians could report the issue from the tool. The report carried current vehicle information into a Zendesk ticket, giving support context for the handoff.

### Physical Guidance — Match instructions to the part in front of technicians

Names alone were not always enough: even internal colleagues struggled to identify an HMI’s “power button.” I drew lettered diagrams for each HMI model, and engineers used those references to arrange the checks.

For sensor tests, I distinguished where technicians should act from where the sensor was located. The aim was to prevent an instruction about movement from being mistaken for the location of a faulty part. These diagrams supported the moments when software guidance had to translate into physical action.

## 6. Real Product Experience

### Follow the bike’s needs through a service visit

This example follows task handling, remaining checks, and reporting. Technicians can choose a different order where the system allows it.

### 1. Connect and review

Automatic checks surface detectable issues and update needs in Workspace. Unchecked items remain distinguishable from known results.

### 2. Choose a task

Open a task to understand the issue and access its update controls or troubleshooting instructions. Technicians initiate the action.

### 3. Complete the remaining checks

Full Diagnosis combines automated checks with guided physical actions. Detected issues are collected and added to Workspace for handling.

### 4. Review progress and report

Workspace prompts the next step as tasks and checks progress. Service reports document completed work and can also include remaining tasks.

## 7. Impact

### Service use at scale after launch

The redesigned Service Tool launched in March 2025 following Beta demonstrations and customer feedback. First-year figures show its operational reach and diagnostic flow completion; they are product outcomes shaped by design, engineering, and service operations together.

- **52,909 distinct vehicles served; 157,412 completion events recorded.** First-year platform figures; events are summed across six categories, not deduplicated tasks.
- **50,314 Full Diagnosis completions.** An event count, separate from the user-based funnel below.
- **93.51% diagnostic funnel completion.** Of 11,995 unique users who started, 11,217 completed the configured three-step funnel within 30 minutes.

These figures describe post-launch use. They do not establish an improvement over the previous tool.


## 8. Reflection

### Build for expansion and the next manual step

Working with marketing and support taught me to design content structures that could grow with the software. A shared structure could accommodate new troubleshooting steps, media, and diagnostic modules. Its later use in Ask AI extended that work beyond the original interface.

Physical maintenance remains harder to guide than automated checks. Full Diagnosis gives technicians steps to follow, but manual testing and updates still need careful support. We continue to explore how AI could offer more immediate guidance at those moments; that remains a direction to investigate.
